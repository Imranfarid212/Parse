# V3.2 — Split-Call Design Review

Reviewed: 2026-07-28
Proposal: header-only hot path, line-items in background, 2.5 Flash Lite as fallback on both
Status: design review only — no code has been modified

---

## Verdict

The shape is right, and it is a better design than the current always-race. Single model on the hot path, hedge only when needed, non-blocking items, per-call fallback — all correct.

Three things will bite you, in descending order of how much they will hurt:

1. **Your hedge threshold is almost certainly below your p50**, which would make the hedge fire on nearly every request. That is worse than today.
2. **The delivery mechanism for line items is unspecified**, and it is the only genuinely hard engineering decision in this plan.
3. **The split creates three new consistency failure modes** the combined call could not have.

None of these are reasons not to do it. All three need a decision before you build.

---

## 1. "Slow after ~250–400ms" — measured how?

This is the highest-risk detail in the proposal.

Your header call will take roughly 450–550ms end to end: ~60 output tokens at ~350 tok/s is ~170ms of generation, plus provider TTFB, plus transport. So if the hedge trigger is **"no complete response after 250–400ms,"** it will be true on the overwhelming majority of requests. You would fire both models nearly every time — paying double, doubling your OpenRouter load, and getting none of the cost saving the split was supposed to buy.

The trigger has to be **time-to-first-token, not elapsed total.** TTFT for flash-lite should be ~150–250ms; no first byte by ~300ms genuinely means the request is stuck. But TTFT requires `stream: true` on the upstream call so you can observe the first chunk. Your current implementation is non-streaming (`callOpenRouter` awaits `response.json()`), which gives you exactly one event — completion — so you cannot distinguish "slow to start" from "generating normally."

Two viable paths:

**(a) Stream upstream, buffer server-side, hedge on TTFT at ~300ms.** Correct, and it does not change your client contract at all — you still return one complete JSON response, you just observe the stream internally. This is much less work than client-side streaming.

**(b) Stay non-streaming, set the hedge delay above your measured p95 header latency.** Simpler. The hedge then only protects the deep tail, which is honestly most of its value anyway.

Either way: **do not ship a guessed constant.** Ship the split with the hedge disabled, measure header-only p50/p90/p95 for a few hundred scans, then set the threshold at your measured p90. Baking in 250–400ms before you have a single header-only measurement is how you end up paying double and not knowing.

Add a `hedge_fired` boolean to your metrics on day one. If you cannot see whether it fires on 5% or 95% of requests, you cannot tune it.

## 2. Both fallbacks are correlated, so they protect less than you think

3.5 Flash Lite and 2.5 Flash Lite both reach Google through the same OpenRouter proxy. A hedge across them protects against **per-request variance** — one stuck request, one bad upstream pick. It does not protect against:

- OpenRouter degradation or rate limiting
- A Google regional issue
- A bad route selection that affects both models equally
- Your own cold-connection cost to `openrouter.ai`, which both calls pay

Provider or route degradation is the more common production incident, and your current design has no answer to it.

For real decorrelation the fallback should differ in at least one dimension beyond model version. The strongest version: **direct Gemini API as primary, OpenRouter 3.5 as the hedge.** That decorrelates the transport as well as the model, and it removes a proxy hop from your hot path. Keep the provider behind your existing abstraction so you can flip per-environment.

If you keep both on OpenRouter, that is a defensible interim choice — just record it as a known gap rather than believing you have redundancy.

---

## 3. 2.5 as fallback is a silent quality downgrade

3.5 is the better extraction model. When the hedge wins, you ship a lower-accuracy header — and the header is the part the user actually reads. A wrong `total` is far more damaging than a slow one.

You already log `timing.winning_model`. Extend it:

- Track **field-correction rate segmented by winning model** — how often the user edits `merchant`, `total`, or `txn_date` when 2.5 won versus 3.5.
- If 2.5's results get materially more corrections, raise the hedge threshold. Waiting 200ms longer for a correct total is the right trade on a header call.

This is the argument for a *higher* threshold than your instinct suggests. Speed on the header only matters because the user is staring at a placeholder; being wrong costs you more than being 200ms later.

## 4. Three new consistency failure modes

The combined call could not produce these. Two independent inferences over the same OCR text can disagree.

### 4.1 Items may not sum to total

Decide explicitly, and write it down:

- **Header `total` is authoritative.** It is the printed total and the number the user checks. Never recompute it from items.
- **Do not surface a mismatch warning by default.** Receipts legitimately have tax, tips, service charges, discounts, and rounding. A warning would fire constantly and train users to ignore it.
- **Do log `sum(items) − total`** as an extraction-quality signal. A drifting distribution tells you something regressed in your prompt or model.

### 4.2 Currency can diverge

Your prompt lets the model override the user's default currency from receipt evidence (city, tax system, symbol). If the items call runs that inference independently, you can end up with an `INR` header and `USD` items.

Fix: **strip currency from the items schema entirely**, and pass the header's resolved currency into the items prompt as a given. Items should never make a currency decision.

### 4.3 Category accuracy may regress

`suggested_category` currently sees the whole receipt including line items. "Printer paper, toner cartridge" is a strong signal for Office Supplies. Header-only, the model sees merchant and total and has to infer from the merchant name alone.

Merchant name usually carries most of the signal, so I would **keep category in the header call** — but this is a real, measurable regression risk you have not accounted for. Log category-correction rate before and after the split. If it degrades meaningfully, move category into the items call and let it update the row asynchronously.

## 5. The unresolved question: how do line items reach the client?

This is the part your plan does not specify, and it is the only hard decision here. Four options:

| Option | How | Trade-off |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **A. Client fires both requests** | Client sends OCR text to two endpoints concurrently | Simplest. Every call stays an ordinary request with ordinary retries. Costs: OCR text sent twice over mobile, two auths (~3ms each once inlined). |
| **B. Server runs items in `waitUntil`, writes `receipt_items`** | Client picks it up via reconciliation or Realtime | No new contract. But `waitUntil` has a wall-clock limit and isolates can be recycled — items can silently never arrive. |
| **C. Client polls a follow-up endpoint** | `GET /extract-items?capture_id=X` | Extra round trips, extra latency, more moving parts. |
| **D. Chunked/streamed response** | Two chunks in one response | This is the streaming contract change you deliberately deferred. |

**I would recommend A.** It preserves the entire reason for choosing the split over streaming: every call remains an ordinary request/response with independent timeouts, independent retries, and no shared server state. The items request failing is trivially retryable from your existing local-row queue. B looks cleaner but reintroduces silent-failure risk on a path you cannot observe from the client, and you would need "items never arrived" recovery anyway — at which point you have built A plus a background job.

The cost of A is sending OCR text twice. It is text, capped at 12k chars in your prompt builder, so this is small.

Whichever you pick, add an **`itemsSyncStatus`** on the local row, mirroring the `imageSyncStatus` pattern you already have. It is the right primitive and you have already proven it works.

## 6. Gate the items call on `is_receipt`

A real win the split unlocks: the header call resolves `is_receipt` and duplicate detection at ~500ms instead of ~2.3s. Use it.

**Fire the items call only after the header returns `is_receipt: true`.**

This costs you ~500ms on the items timeline — items land ~2.2s instead of ~1.65s — and buys you:

- No wasted model spend on non-receipts and duplicates
- No abort plumbing, and no input tokens burned on a call you cancel mid-flight
- Simpler failure semantics

Since items are non-blocking and the screen is already useful at ~1s, that extra 500ms costs the user essentially nothing. **Sequential-gated is the better trade here**, and it is the opposite of the instinct to parallelise everything.

If you do go parallel, the items request must be genuinely abortable and actually aborted on `is_receipt: false` — and you will still pay input tokens on the aborted call.

---

## 7. Separate budgets, and items failure must be non-fatal

Two calls need two policies. Do not share one.

**Header call:**
- Wall-clock deadline ~2s total, including any hedge, propagated as an `AbortSignal`
- Failure is fatal to the scan → falls into your existing `llm_failed_retryable` queue
- Aggressive: this is what the user is waiting for

**Items call:**
- Generous deadline, ~8s
- Retryable in the background with jittered backoff
- **Failure is non-fatal.** A receipt with a correct merchant, date, total, and category and no line items is still a useful receipt.

That last point needs to be true in the data model and the UI, not just in intent. Make sure a receipt with zero items is a valid, non-error state everywhere — the row, the review screen, the export. Right now `rejectEmptyExtraction` only trips when merchant is empty *and* total is 0 *and* items are empty, so it will not misfire on a good header response. But verify that assumption holds once you have a header-only schema in play.

One concrete mechanical note: you cannot reuse `extractionJsonSchema` for the header call. `line_items` is in its `required` array with `strict: true`, so the model would be obliged to emit items and you would save nothing. You need a second schema object with `line_items` removed from both `properties` and `required`.

## 8. Metrics to add before you tune anything

| Field | Why |
| --- | --- |
| `header_ttfb_ms` | The only honest hedge trigger |
| `header_total_ms` | Sets the hedge threshold |
| `header_model` | Which model actually served the header |
| `hedge_fired` | Tells you if the threshold is wrong — most likely thing to be wrong |
| `hedge_won` | Tells you if hedging is earning its cost |
| `items_total_ms` | Items timeline |
| `items_model` | Quality segmentation |
| `items_outcome` | `ok` / `timeout` / `failed` / `skipped_not_receipt` |
| `items_sum_minus_total` | Extraction-quality drift signal |
| `provider_ttfb_ms` / `generation_ms` | Separates connection cost from inference |

`hedge_fired` and `header_ttfb_ms` are the two that matter most. Without them you are tuning a constant blind.

---

## 9. Recommended sequencing

1. **Inline the JWKS first.** Auth is 833ms; the entire header call is projected at ~500ms. Fixing the split before the auth makes no sense — auth is the bigger number and the easier fix.
2. **Build the split with the hedge disabled.** One model, header-only, items via option A. Measure.
3. **Collect a few hundred scans.** Get real p50/p90/p95 on `header_ttfb_ms` and `header_total_ms`.
4. **Then set the hedge threshold** at measured p90, and decide streaming-vs-static based on what the distribution looks like.
5. **Decorrelate the fallback** — direct Gemini primary, OpenRouter hedge — once the split is stable.

Steps 1 and 2 get you under 2s. Steps 3–5 are what make it hold at p95.

## Projected outcome

| | Now | After split (hedge off) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Local draft visible | 0.48s | 0.48s |
| Confirmed header fields | 2.83s | **~1.0s** |
| Line items (gated on `is_receipt`) | 2.83s | ~2.2s |
| Non-receipt rejection | 2.83s | **~1.0s** |
| Models called per scan | 2 always | 1 typically |

The non-receipt row is the underrated one. Rejecting a bad scan in 1s instead of 2.8s is a bigger perceived-quality improvement than it looks on paper, because that path currently feels like the app hung before telling the user off.
